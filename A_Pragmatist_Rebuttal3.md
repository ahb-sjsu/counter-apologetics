# Four Deductive Arguments for God Have Not Been Shown to Compel Assent

**Andrew H. Bond**
**San Jose State University**

---

## Abstract

Four deductive arguments for the existence of God are often presented as rationally compelling. They are the Kalam cosmological argument, the contingency argument, the moral argument and the modal ontological argument. This paper argues that each one rests on a single premise whose support, as its leading defenders present it, has not been shown to meet the standard Craig sets for a good deductive argument, and it makes the case by standards that a metaphysical realist accepts. The load-bearing premises are the universal causal principle, the Principle of Sufficient Reason, the claim that stance-independent value requires God, and the claim that a maximally great being is possible. Each is supported by intuition that has not been tested in the domain the premise concerns, and the arguments supply no further justification for that extension. The rival premises differ in strength. Rejecting the Principle of Sufficient Reason, or holding that moral facts need no divine ground, blocks the route to God without implying that God does not exist. For the ontological argument, apparent coherence supports a rival possibility premise no less than the original, and under the same modal assumptions the rival implies that no maximally great being exists. The paper then offers a pragmatist account of why these premises seem self-evident to their defenders. That account is separable from the main argument, and a reader who rejects pragmatism can accept the main argument without it.

**Keywords:** philosophy of religion, natural theology, Kalam cosmological argument, Principle of Sufficient Reason, moral argument, sceptical theism, modal ontological argument, pragmatism

# 1. Introduction

Contemporary analytic natural theology defends deductive arguments for the existence of God. Craig (2008), Pruss (2009), Koons (1997) and Plantinga (1974) offer arguments that are meant to do more than raise the probability of theism. Craig has stated the standard he applies in two forms. In 2008 he held that a good deductive argument is valid and has premises each of which is more plausible than its denial (Craig 2008, 51–56, at 55). In 2016 he accepted, following McGrew and DePoe (2013), that this condition is inadequate, because each premise can be more probable than not while their conjunction is not. His revised condition is that the conjunction of the premises be more probable than not, since only then is the conclusion guaranteed to be more probable than not (Craig 2016). He also distinguishes an argument that is good in this sense from one whose conclusion is probable enough to warrant belief. The four arguments are valid. The dispute is about the premises.

This paper examines the four arguments as presented by Craig (2008), Craig and Sinclair (2009), Pruss (2009) and Plantinga (1974). It argues that each depends on one premise that these presentations have not shown to be more plausible than its denial for an inquirer who does not already accept the conclusion. A conjunction is never more probable than any of its conjuncts, so each argument as presented has then not been shown to meet Craig's revised standard, any more than his earlier one. The argument for this claim uses three questions that a realist can accept. What supports the premise? Has that kind of support been tested in the domain the premise concerns? Has the support presented been shown to favour the premise over a rival, and what does the rival imply? Section 2 states these questions. Sections 3 to 6 apply them to the Kalam, contingency, moral and ontological arguments in turn.

The conclusion is limited. The paper does not argue that God does not exist, and it does not argue that any of the four load-bearing premises is false. It argues that none of them has been shown to be more plausible than its denial, so none of the four arguments, as presented, has been shown to be good by Craig's standard or to compel assent.

Several of the individual objections below are already in the literature. Oppy (2006) argues at length that no argument about the existence of God should persuade every reasonable participant in the debate. The present paper adds three things. It identifies the same kind of shortfall in all four arguments, namely intuition extended to a domain where it has not been tested, and it uses a result about compression to show that success in tested cases does not, by itself, establish reliability in untested ones. It applies a symmetry argument about moral knowledge to Craig's identification of God with the Good. It then offers, in Section 7, a pragmatist account of why intuitions of this kind are so readily mistaken for necessary truths. Sections 3 to 6 do not depend on Section 7. A realist who rejects pragmatism can accept Sections 3 to 6 and reject Section 7.

Section 8 replies to objections and Section 9 concludes.

# 2. A good deductive argument needs premises supported independently of its conclusion

A valid argument transmits whatever support its premises have to its conclusion and adds none. An argument for theism therefore persuades a non-theist only if its premises are better supported, for that person, than the denial of its conclusion. Craig's revised criterion, that the conjunction of the premises be more probable than not, expresses this requirement. A single premise that is not shown to be more plausible than its denial prevents the conjunction from meeting it.

Three questions test whether a premise meets the criterion.

The first question asks what supports the premise. In the four arguments considered here the answer is usually rational intuition, sometimes supplemented by induction from familiar cases.

The second question asks whether that kind of support has been tested in the domain the premise concerns. Intuitions and inductive generalizations are checked against the cases in which they were formed. A premise about the beginning of time, about the totality of contingent facts, or about what exists in every possible world concerns cases outside every case in which the supporting intuition was checked. This does not show that the intuition is unreliable in those cases. It shows that its reliability there is untested. An untested extension is not a defeater in the way a record of failure would be. It removes the corroboration that would otherwise distinguish the intuition from a rival, and it leaves the extension in need of a justification that the argument must supply.

A result about compression shows why success in tested cases need not carry over to untested ones. Consider a code designed to serve one user of a signal. Such a code keeps what that user needs and may discard the rest. Suppose a second user later relies on the same code. If the second user needs features that the code kept but weighted differently, the extra cost is bounded. If the second user needs a feature that the code discarded, the second user's error has a floor that no further refinement of the code can lower, and nothing inside the code indicates that the floor exists (Bond 2026a, Theorem 'Omission floor'). A code tuned to its user can also serve that user better than a faithful copy of the signal would while reproducing the signal worse (Bond 2026b). These are results about engineered codes, not about minds, and they are offered here as a model of the second question rather than as evidence about human cognition. On that model, the success of a faculty in the cases that shaped it is evidence about its performance on what those cases required. That success belongs to the faculty acting in its environment as a whole, so it does not show that each feature the faculty represents is represented accurately, since errors in different features can offset one another. It is evidence about untested cases only to the extent that they depend on features correlated with the features retained. A code that keeps one of two fair coin flips predicts that flip perfectly. It predicts the discarded flip no better than chance if the two flips are independent, and better than chance if they are correlated. The omission floor concerns the part of a discarded feature that is independent of what was kept. Transfer to untested cases is therefore possible, but it requires a justification, namely evidence that the untested feature is tied to what was tested. Success in the tested cases does not by itself supply that evidence. Stich (1990) and Hoffman, Singh and Prakash (2015) argue that selection for usefulness need not select for accuracy. The model adds an asymmetry. A faculty that weights a relevant feature wrongly pays a bounded cost, but a faculty that never encoded a feature cannot recover the part of it that is independent of what it did encode, by any amount of further effort on what it did encode.

The third question asks whether the support presented for a premise has been shown to favour it over a rival, and what the rival implies. Rivals come in two strengths. A blocking rival is one whose truth leaves the argument's conclusion unsupported without implying its denial. A contrary rival implies the denial of the conclusion under the same assumptions the argument uses. Support that is compatible with both the premise and its rival may still favour one of them, so compatibility alone settles nothing. The question is whether the support presented has been shown to favour the original premise. Where it has not, the premise is not shown to be more plausible than its denial unless further support is given. Only a contrary rival supports the claim that the conclusion is false, and this paper draws no such claim from a blocking rival.

None of these questions assumes anti-realism about logic, modality or value. A realist who holds that rational intuition gives prima facie evidence (Huemer 2005) can accept all three. The realist holds that intuition is defeasible evidence. The third question asks only whether the support presented has been shown to favour the premise over a rival, and answering it requires no claim that the rival is equally supported. The second question identifies something weaker than a defeater, namely the absence of the corroboration that a tested domain would provide.

# 3. The Kalam's causal premise is extended beyond every case that supports it

Craig's Kalam argument (Craig 2008, 111–156; Craig and Sinclair 2009) runs as follows.

1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

Craig then argues that the cause must be timeless, spaceless, immaterial and personal. This section grants premise 2 for the sake of argument and examines premise 1.

Craig supports premise 1 in two ways. He holds that it is a metaphysical intuition, that something cannot come from nothing, and he holds that it is confirmed by every observed case (Craig and Sinclair 2009, 182–187; Craig 2008, 111–112). The inductive support covers events within time that arise from prior physical conditions. The beginning of the universe, as premise 2 describes it, would be an event with no prior time and no prior physical conditions. Every confirming instance therefore differs from the case to which premise 1 is applied in exactly the respects that make that case special. Induction from the familiar cases supports a principle restricted to things that begin within an existing universe. It does not support the unrestricted principle the argument needs.

The intuitive support is open to a similar objection. Intuitions about causation have been revised before when they were extended to unfamiliar physical domains. For two centuries many physicists and philosophers treated it as evident that every event has a prior state that fixes it uniquely. On standard interpretations of quantum mechanics the timing of a radioactive decay is not fixed by any prior state. This does not show that such events are uncaused. Craig and Sinclair (2009, 182–183) reply that even on an indeterministic reading quantum events have causes. Virtual particles, for example, arise from the vacuum, which they describe as an indeterministic cause of their origination. They also point out that deterministic interpretations such as Bohm's (1952) remain available, so quantum mechanics does not show that the world is indeterministic. The revised principle is also stronger than Craig's premise, which requires only a cause and not a cause that fixes the outcome. Its revision therefore does not show that Craig's premise is false, and it does not show that causal intuitions are unreliable in general. It shows something narrower. A causal principle that was widely held to be evident turned out to be optional in a domain unlike the one in which it was formed, so felt obviousness has not by itself guaranteed causal principles when they were extended to unfamiliar domains. That is a reason to ask for justification beyond felt obviousness before the weaker premise is extended to the beginning of time itself.

The step from a cause of the universe to a personal cause adds a further extension. Craig argues that only a free agent could produce a temporal effect from a timeless cause. Morriston (2000) argues that the notion of agency in play depends on deliberation and choice in time, and that no account has been given of what a timeless choice would be. The intuition that only an agent could explain the beginning of the universe is formed entirely from agents who act in time.

The result is that premise 1 is supported in its restricted form, while its extension to the beginning of time rests on intuition alone and needs a justification that the argument does not supply. This is not a reason to believe that the universe began without a cause. It is a reason to deny that premise 1, in the scope the argument needs, has been shown to be more plausible than its denial.

# 4. The Principle of Sufficient Reason faces an objection that realists treat as serious

The contingency argument (Pruss 2009, 24–25) relies on the Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR), the claim that every contingent fact has an explanation. From the PSR it infers that the totality of contingent facts has an explanation that is not itself contingent, and so a necessary being exists.

The PSR is contested inside realist metaphysics, and the strongest objection to it does not depend on any view about the status of logic. Van Inwagen (1983, 202–204) considers the conjunction of all contingent truths. If the PSR holds, this conjunction has an explanation. The explanation cannot be a contingent truth, since every contingent truth is a conjunct of what is to be explained, and a conjunction is not explained by one of its own parts. The explanation must therefore be a necessary truth. If an explanation entails what it explains, then the conjunction of all contingent truths is entailed by a necessary truth and is itself necessary. Every truth is then necessary and nothing is contingent. Defenders of the argument reject the conclusion that nothing is contingent, so they must reject either the unrestricted PSR or the requirement that explanations entail what they explain.

Pruss (2009, 50–57) takes the second option, and his reply does not rest on a special exception for God. Ordinary explanations often do not entail what they explain. Scientific explanations in general do not give conditions that entail what they explain, and statistical explanations are the clearest case (Pruss 2009, 52). A stochastic explanation of a radioactive decay cites the prior state of the nucleus and a law that assigns a probability to the decay, and it explains the decay without entailing it. On a libertarian account of free choice, an explanation of an action by the agent's reasons makes the action intelligible without entailing it. Pruss holds that the conjunction of all contingent truths can be explained in the same way, by the necessary proposition that a necessarily existing God freely chose what to create while impressed by some reason (Pruss 2009, 86). Van Inwagen's argument then fails, because its step from a necessary explanation to a necessary explanandum assumes entailment.

The question this leaves is whether a non-entailing explanation of this kind covers the totality of contingent facts. In the ordinary cases the explanation works against a background that it does not have to account for. The stochastic explanation takes the nucleus and the law as given. The explanation by reasons takes the agent and the agent's situation as given. In the case of the totality, the choice is itself a contingent fact and so belongs to what is to be explained. On Pruss's account it is explained by a necessary proposition about God's choosing for a reason, and that proposition is compatible with a different choice. Whether a necessary truth that is compatible with either outcome explains why one outcome is actual is the point in dispute between Pruss and his critics (see Oppy 2006, 130–137, on the Gale and Pruss argument). The PSR receives its intuitive support from ordinary cases, where such a background is present. Its application to the one case with no background is an extension of the kind identified in Section 2, and it needs a justification that the intuition from ordinary cases does not provide.

The rival premise is that some contingent fact, or the totality of contingent facts, has no explanation. This premise is not obviously less plausible than the PSR in its strong form. Each view ends explanation at some point. The theist ends it at a necessary being whose existence and nature need no further explanation. The rival ends it at a brute fact. Calling the theist's stopping point necessary does not remove the question of why reality includes a necessary being of that particular nature rather than some other necessary foundation. The argument therefore does not show that its premise is more plausible than its rival. The rival is a blocking rival in the sense of Section 2. A brute contingent fact at the base of explanation is consistent with the existence of God, so accepting the rival removes this route to God without implying that God does not exist. If the argument succeeds on its own terms it shows only that some necessary foundation exists, and further argument is needed to identify that foundation with God.

# 5. The moral argument faces two separate objections and a symmetry problem

Craig's moral argument (Craig 2008, 172–183) runs as follows.

1. If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist.
2. Objective moral values and duties do exist.
3. Therefore, God exists.

Craig defines objective moral values as values that hold independently of whether any human being believes them to hold (Craig 2008, 173). He uses this definition in both premises. The argument does not equivocate on the word "objective," and a critic who accepts Craig's definition must challenge one of the premises directly. Two different challenges are available. They should be kept apart because they require different commitments.

## 5.1 Denying premise 2 requires a substantive metaethical commitment

The first challenge denies premise 2 in Craig's sense. Craig supports premise 2 by appeal to moral experience, for example the experience that torturing a child for fun is wrong. That experience is strong evidence that the act is wrong. It is weaker evidence about what makes it wrong. Constructivist and response-dependent accounts treat moral claims as robust, non-arbitrary and binding without treating them as independent of all human attitudes (Street 2006). On such accounts the claim that torturing a child for fun is wrong is true, and every competent participant in moral practice would converge on it. Moral experience does not by itself discriminate between such an account and Craig's.

This challenge offers an alternative account of moral objectivity. It does not refute premise 2 under Craig's definition. A critic who takes this route accepts the burden of defending a stance-dependent metaethics, and many realists will not grant it. The point needed here is narrower. Premise 2, in the sense the argument requires, is a contested metaethical thesis. It is not something that ordinary moral experience establishes, so the argument's support for premise 2 is weaker than its appeal to moral experience suggests.

## 5.2 Accepting stance-independent value leaves premise 1 unsupported

The second challenge accepts premise 2 in Craig's sense and denies premise 1. Godless normative realism holds that there are stance-independent moral facts and that the most basic of them are necessary truths that need no external ground (Shafer-Landau 2003; Enoch 2011; Wielenberg 2014). On this view moral facts are like mathematical facts as many realists conceive them. They hold necessarily and are not made true by any being.

Craig objects that on this view moral facts would float free without explanation. The objection applies to the theistic view as well. On Craig's view the basic moral fact is that God's nature is good, and that fact is necessary and has no further explanation. Both views end in a necessary moral fact that is not explained by anything else. Premise 1 asserts that only the theistic stopping point is acceptable, and the argument gives no reason why this should be so. This is the same structure found in the contingency argument (Section 4). A realist about value can therefore deny premise 1 without giving up stance-independence. Godless normative realism is a blocking rival in the sense of Section 2. It is consistent with the existence of God and implies only that stance-independent value does not require God.

## 5.3 Identifying God with the Good requires a moral judgment that sceptical theism undermines

The Euthyphro dilemma poses a familiar problem for divine command theory. If an act is right because God commands it, rightness seems arbitrary. If God commands an act because it is right, rightness seems independent of God. Craig's reply is that God's commands flow from God's nature and that God's nature is the standard of goodness (Craig 2008, 181–183). On this reply goodness is neither arbitrary nor independent of God.

The reply settles what goodness is by definition. It does not settle which being, if any, meets the definition. The moral argument concludes that God exists, where God is the being of a particular theistic tradition. To identify the ground of value with that being, one must judge that the being's character and acts are good. That judgment uses ordinary moral competence. One examines what the being is said to do and judges that it is good.

Many defenders of theism also hold sceptical theism (Bergmann 2001). Sceptical theism says that human moral judgment is too limited to infer, from the fact that we see no justifying reason for an apparently bad divine act or permission, that there is no such reason. The space of possible goods and reasons may extend beyond what humans can survey.

The claim defended here is narrow. A limitation invoked to defeat adverse moral evidence must also be applied to favourable evidence of the same kind. The adverse evidence in question is an inference from the observed character of divine acts to the character of the agent, made without knowledge of the agent's full aims. Favourable evidence of the same kind is the inference from apparently good divine acts to a good character. If the space of unknown reasons can contain a justification for apparent evil, then the space of unknown aims can contain an aim that is not good behind apparent goodness, and our failure to see such an aim is no better evidence of its absence (Howard-Snyder 2009; Wielenberg 2010). Law (2010) develops a related symmetry between a good god and an evil god. This does not show that every kind of evidence for divine goodness is defeated. It shows that evidence of this one kind cannot be discounted on one side and counted on the other.

The question is then whether the theist has evidence for divine goodness that is not of this kind. A sceptical theist may hold that knowledge of God's goodness comes from sources other than inference from observed acts. Bergmann (2009, 389) concedes that sceptical theism undermines some theistic arguments and mentions divine revelation as another way of learning that something is good all things considered. Three candidates are common. The first is the definition of God as a maximally perfect being. It makes it true by definition that God, if God exists, is good. It does not show that the being described by a particular tradition satisfies the definition, and the moral argument needs that link to reach a theistic conclusion rather than the conclusion that some necessary ground of value exists. The second is religious experience in which God's goodness seems evident. Such an experience presents an appearance of benevolence. Taken as evidence about the agent's character as a whole, it is subject to the same limitation as the inference from apparently good acts. The third is revelation or testimony that God is good. Testimony from God is evidence only if God does not deceive, and whether God has reasons beyond human ken to deceive is a question of the kind that sceptical theism declares unanswerable (Wielenberg 2010). Each candidate either fails to connect the definition to a particular being or falls within the scope of the limitation. This does not exclude a source of evidence that escapes the symmetry. It shows that the moral argument does not supply one, and that a sceptical theist who identifies the God of a particular tradition with the Good owes an account of what that source is.

# 6. The ontological argument is valid, and its possibility premise lacks independent support

The modal ontological argument (Plantinga 1974, 213–217) can be stated with G for "a maximally great being exists." Maximal greatness includes necessary existence, so it is necessarily true that G implies that G is necessary. The argument runs as follows.

1. Possibly G.
2. Necessarily, if G then necessarily G.
3. Therefore, G.

The argument is valid in S5, and indeed in any modal logic that contains the B principle, which says that whatever is true is necessarily possibly true. From premises 1 and 2 it follows that possibly necessarily G, and in any such logic this implies G. The result holds in every model of the premises. It does not depend on a convenient choice of model.

Some realists deny that S5 is the logic of metaphysical modality (Salmon 1989). This does not provide an easy escape, because the argument needs only B. A critic who blocks the argument this way must deny B for metaphysical modality, which is a contested thesis in its own right. The objection below does not depend on it.

The dispute concerns premise 1. Craig and Plantinga mean metaphysical possibility, not epistemic possibility. Craig explicitly states that the fact that we cannot rule G out, or that we detect no contradiction in it, does not make it metaphysically possible (Craig 2008, 185). The objection that he confuses the two therefore misdescribes his position. The relevant question is what supports the claim of metaphysical possibility.

The usual support is that the concept of a maximally great being appears coherent. Apparent coherence is evidence of epistemic possibility. Craig himself grants that epistemic possibility is not enough, so this evidence does not by itself support the premise as he intends it.

A rival premise is supported in the same way. Consider the premise that possibly no maximally great being exists. It is also conceivable, and no contradiction has been detected in it either. Combined with premise 2 it yields the conclusion that no maximally great being exists, and in S5 that this is necessary. This rival is a contrary rival in the sense of Section 2. Under the modal assumptions the argument itself uses, it implies that the argument's conclusion is false. The two possibility premises cannot both be true. The deductions from each are valid, and both have been checked formally (Note 4). The comparison of their support is a separate, philosophical question that no formal check settles. That neither premise has been shown to contain a contradiction does not make them equally warranted, since a defender may have further grounds for one of them. It does show that apparent coherence alone supplies no demonstrated basis for preferring the theistic premise. Any such basis must come from an argument for the premise that does not rest on its apparent coherence. Plantinga (1974, 219–221) concedes that a rational person can reject premise 1, and he presents the argument as showing the rational acceptability of theism rather than as a proof.

An independent argument for premise 1 would change this assessment. Gödel's ontological proof can be read as an attempt to supply one, by deriving the possibility of a God-like being from axioms about positive properties (Gödel 1970/1995). Two versions must be distinguished. Using the higher-order prover Leo-II, Benzmüller and Woltzenlogel Paleo (2016) found that the axioms of Gödel's own 1970 manuscript are inconsistent, because his definition of essence omits a condition that Scott later added. Scott's variant is consistent, and it entails modal collapse, the claim that every truth is a necessary truth (Sobel 1987). Benzmüller and Woltzenlogel Paleo (2014) confirmed the consistency of Scott's variant and its modal collapse with automated theorem provers. Benzmüller (2026) has since re-proved the inconsistency of the 1970 axioms in the Lean 4 proof assistant and reports that Scott's necessary existence and modal collapse require only a symmetric accessibility relation, the same frame condition that suffices for the argument of this section. The collapse is not a feature of some unusual model. It holds in every model of Scott's axioms, so anyone who accepts them must accept that nothing is contingent. Axioms that seemed plausible as statements about positive properties were inconsistent in one version and eliminated contingency in the other. Emendations by Anderson (1990) and Fitting (2002) avoid the collapse, but their axioms are again supported by intuition about positive properties. The history of Gödel's proof is therefore evidence that intuitions about maximal perfection are unreliable guides to what is metaphysically possible.

# 7. A pragmatist account explains why these premises seem self-evident

Sections 3 to 6 identified the same shortfall in four arguments. In each case an intuition that functions well in familiar cases is applied to a case outside its domain, and in three of the four cases an equally intuitive rival premise points the other way. This section offers an explanation of why intelligent defenders find the load-bearing premises self-evident. The explanation draws on pragmatism, and a reader who rejects pragmatism can accept the preceding sections without it.

## 7.1 Logic and modal reasoning are tools calibrated by their uses

In the pragmatist tradition logical forms are patterns of successful inquiry rather than descriptions of a mind-independent order (Dewey 1938, 101–119). Quine (1951) argued that no statement, including a logical law, is immune to revision in principle. Putnam (1981, 49–74) developed a view on which truth is idealized rational acceptability within a conceptual scheme. On this family of views, logic, mathematics and modal semantics are instruments. They are shaped by the problems they were developed to solve and are judged by how well they solve them.

If this view is correct, the four arguments share a common pattern. An inferential habit that works well in a familiar domain, such as looking for causes, demanding explanations, judging cruelty wrong or testing concepts for coherence, is codified as a principle. The principle is then treated as a description of how reality must be in every domain. The existence and nature of God are then read off from the principle in a domain where its reliability has never been checked. The pragmatist identifies the third step as the error. The neutral version of the same diagnosis, given in Sections 3 to 6, says only that the third step has not been justified.

## 7.2 Quantum mechanics shows that intuitions about physical properties can require revision

Birkhoff and von Neumann (1936) showed that the propositions describing a quantum system, represented by the closed subspaces of a Hilbert space, form a lattice in which the distributive law fails. Kochen and Specker (1967) showed that, in Hilbert spaces of dimension three or more, no assignment of definite values to all quantities is consistent with the functional relations among compatible quantities and independent of which quantities are measured together.

These results concern the mathematical structure that represents physical properties. They do not show that the reasoning physicists use to study that structure is non-classical. Theorems about Hilbert spaces are proved in classical logic. Putnam (1969) argued that quantum mechanics requires a revision of logic itself. He later concluded that his quantum-logical interpretation was unworkable (Putnam 1994), and he set it aside on that ground in his later discussion of quantum mechanics (Putnam 2005). The results support a narrower point. It once seemed evident that every physical quantity has a definite value whether or not it is measured. That intuition had to be given up or heavily qualified in a domain unlike the one in which it was formed. This is an instance of the pattern described in Section 3. It is not evidence that classical logic fails in general.

## 7.3 Gödel's incompleteness theorems do not bear on these arguments

Gödel's incompleteness theorems are sometimes invoked against arguments of this kind. They do not bear on the four arguments. The theorems apply to consistent, effectively axiomatized theories that can express a modest amount of arithmetic. They show that such a theory leaves some sentences undecided and cannot prove its own consistency. They do not show that such a theory is unsound, that its theorems fail to describe their subject matter, or that a deductive argument cannot establish its conclusion (Franzén 2005, 167–172). The modal ontological argument uses propositional modal logic, which does not express arithmetic, and the consistency of its premises can be shown directly with a model containing a single world. Incompleteness therefore gives no reason to doubt any of the four arguments. The objections in Sections 3 to 6 do not depend on it.

# 8. Objections and replies

## 8.1 Rational intuition is evidence, so the premises are supported

*Objection.* Rational intuitions are prima facie evidence. A premise that seems true after careful reflection is justified unless there is a defeater. The load-bearing premises seem true to many careful thinkers, so they are justified.

*Reply.* Sections 3 to 6 accept that intuitions are prima facie evidence. For the contingency, moral and ontological arguments they identify a rival and find that the support presented has not been shown to favour the premise over it. Prima facie evidence that has not been shown to favour a premise over its rival does not show the premise to be more plausible than its denial. For the Kalam they identify something weaker, namely that the causal intuition has not been tested in the domain to which it is applied. The objector may reply that careful reflection strengthens the intuition. Reflection works on what a faculty already encodes, and it can extract implications from information the faculty retains. The model of Section 2 rules out something narrower. Reflection cannot recover information that is absent from a fixed representation. The model does not settle whether a judgment about the beginning of time requires information of that kind, and this paper does not claim to have settled it. The reply is therefore limited. If the judgment requires information the faculty does not retain, reflection cannot supply it. If the judgment draws only on retained information, reflection can still go wrong, so whether it improves reliability needs a separate assessment. In neither case does greater confidence after reflection show by itself that the extension examined in Section 3 is justified.

## 8.2 The argument refutes itself because it relies on logic

*Objection.* The paper uses logic to argue that logic cannot be trusted.

*Reply.* The paper does not argue that logic is untrustworthy. Every argument in Sections 3 to 6 is valid in classical logic, and the paper grants that the ontological argument is valid as well. The paper's claim concerns premises, not inference rules. It argues that certain substantive premises are supported by intuitions whose reliability in the relevant domain has not been shown. Section 7 adds that the pragmatist regards logic as a tool. Using a tool in the domain where it has been checked is consistent with doubting its application to a domain where it has not.

## 8.3 The argument also undermines science

*Objection.* If intuitions extended beyond their domain are suspect, then so is much of physics, which extends mathematics to unobserved scales.

*Reply.* Physics extends its principles to new domains and then tests them there. The extension of Newtonian mechanics to high velocities was tested and failed, and the extension of quantum mechanics to the scale of atoms was tested and succeeded. The load-bearing premises in Sections 3 to 6 cannot be tested in the domain to which they are applied. The beginning of time, the totality of contingent facts, the grounds of value and the full space of metaphysical possibilities offer no observations against which the premises could be checked. The objection identifies a real similarity, namely that both science and natural theology extend principles beyond their origin. The difference is that science checks its extensions and these arguments cannot. Premise 2 of the Kalam is the exception, since it draws on cosmology, and this paper granted it for that reason.

## 8.4 The effectiveness of mathematics points to a rational creator

*Objection.* Wigner (1960) described the effectiveness of mathematics in physics as unreasonable. A designed universe explains this effectiveness better than any pragmatist account does.

*Reply.* Part of the effectiveness is explained by selection. Mathematics that failed to describe physical phenomena, such as Euclidean geometry applied to strong gravitational fields, was replaced. Adams (2002, 131) describes a puddle that admires how well its hole fits it. The puddle analogy does not explain why some mathematics developed for purely internal reasons, such as complex numbers and group theory, later proved essential to physics. That remains a real puzzle for any account. The paper does not need to solve it. The objection supports a design argument, which is abductive. It does not supply support for any of the four deductive premises examined here.

## 8.5 Evolutionary naturalism undermines the reliability of the critic's own reasoning

*Objection.* Plantinga (1993, ch. 12) argues that if our cognitive faculties arose by unguided evolution, we have no reason to trust them. The pragmatist account in Section 7 appeals to evolutionary and social origins and so undermines itself.

*Reply.* The objection targets Section 7 only, since Sections 3 to 6 make no appeal to evolution. Part of Plantinga's premise can be granted. Selection favours faculties that serve behaviour, and the compression results cited in Section 2 show concretely that a representation tuned to a task can serve the task better while representing its source worse (Bond 2026b). What follows is narrower than either global reliability or global unreliability. Success at a task supports the faculty's performance on that task. Whether the faculty also represents its source accurately, even within the task, requires its own assessment, since the same results show that a useful representation can misdescribe its source. Fitelson and Sober (1998) argue that Plantinga's estimate of the probability that our faculties are reliable ignores our evidence about how they have performed, and that evidence concerns the tested domains. The pragmatist account claims tested performance on tested tasks and no more, and it is the extension of these faculties beyond the tested tasks that Sections 3 to 6 question.

# 9. Conclusion

Each of the four principal deductive arguments for the existence of God depends on one premise that is supported mainly by intuition. In each case the intuition is applied to a domain where its reliability has not been checked. For the Kalam, the causal principle is supported only in its restricted form. For the contingency argument, the strong PSR faces van Inwagen's objection, and Pruss's reply, which appeals to the ordinary fact that explanations need not entail what they explain, leaves open whether such an explanation covers the totality of contingent facts. For the moral argument, a critic can deny that stance-independent value exists or deny that it requires God, and the identification of God with the Good depends on a moral judgment that sceptical theism undermines. For the ontological argument, apparent coherence supplies no demonstrated basis for preferring the possibility premise to its rival, and the rival implies the contrary conclusion. For the contingency and moral arguments, the rivals block the route to God without implying that God does not exist.

None of this shows that God does not exist or that any of the premises is false. It shows that none of the four arguments, as presented by Craig (2008), Craig and Sinclair (2009), Pruss (2009) and Plantinga (1974), has been shown to meet Craig's standard, in either its 2008 or its 2016 form, for an inquirer who does not already accept theism. A non-theist may still accept particular premises on independent grounds. The claim concerns the adequacy of the justification these presentations supply. The pragmatist account in Section 7 offers one explanation of why these premises nonetheless seem self-evident. That account can be rejected without affecting the main conclusion. Theism may still be supported by other kinds of consideration, including abductive, experiential and practical ones. It has not been established by these presentations of the deductive arguments.
